\section{Security Implications}
\label{sec:implications}
% =============================================================================

\subsection{Dual Use: Reconnaissance and Auditing}

DPRI can be computed in minutes from raw data, without training a model.
This makes it dual-use. An adversary with access to several candidate
datasets, for example a public benchmark suite or a leaked schema, can use
DPRI to decide which to attack, focusing effort on the highest-uniqueness,
highest-risk targets rather than attacking blindly. Because DPRI requires no
model and no queries, this reconnaissance is essentially free.

Consider a concrete scenario. A research consortium publishes a suite of
de-identified hospital datasets, and models trained on them are served
through an API. An adversary planning a membership campaign against the
API computes DPRI on each published dataset in minutes, observes that
one sits in the high-uniqueness tail, and concentrates its shadow-model
budget there, where our measurements say the expected AUC is highest.
Nothing in this reconnaissance touches the victim's infrastructure, so
no monitoring can observe it. The mitigation is correspondingly
data-side rather than network-side: treat the geometric profile of a
dataset as part of its release review, and pair high-DPRI releases with
the safeguards below.

The same property serves defenders, and the asymmetry favors them. A
data holder can compute DPRI before releasing a dataset or a model
trained on it. A high score, driven by high sample uniqueness or
dimensionality, signals that the data is intrinsically more exposed and
should trigger stronger safeguards: data minimization, stricter access
controls, deduplication and outlier review (mindful that removing the
most exposed records shifts exposure to the next
layer~\cite{carlini2022onion}), or a tighter differential-privacy
budget. The defender computes DPRI once at collection time; the
attacker still has to mount the actual attack.

\subsection{Using DPRI in Practice}

A deployment workflow follows directly from how the index was
validated. At data-collection or acquisition time, the holder runs the
auditor (Algorithm~\ref{alg:dpri}) on the standardized feature matrix,
at the cost of minutes on a laptop. The resulting score is interpreted
by rank, not by absolute value: our tool ships the feature distributions
of the 31-dataset reference corpus, and a new dataset is placed against
them, with the high-uniqueness, low-density tail flagged for review.
Because the prediction is a moderate ranking signal
(Section~\ref{sec:results}), the flag is a triage signal, not a
verdict: a flagged dataset warrants a direct MIA evaluation before
release, while an unflagged one can proceed under default safeguards.
Finally, the score and its distributional breakdown belong in the
dataset's documentation alongside its provenance and intended
use~\cite{gebru2021datasheets}, so that downstream trainers inherit the
risk assessment rather than recomputing or, worse, ignoring it. For MIA
researchers the same profile serves a different purpose: reporting the
uniqueness and density of an evaluation dataset alongside attack
numbers would make results comparable across papers, and benchmark
suites should span the DPRI range rather than concentrate in its upper
tail (Section~\ref{sec:finding2}).

\subsection{Toward Data-Aware Differential Privacy}

Our results suggest, but do not establish, that a fixed differential-privacy
budget protects different datasets unequally, because the underlying risk
differs before any noise is added. This is consistent with two known
observations: that the empirical protection delivered by a given
$\varepsilon$ varies widely in practice~\cite{jayaraman2019evaluating},
and that the cost of private learning depends strongly on how the data is
represented~\cite{tramer2021differentially}. A natural direction is a
data-aware budget that tightens $\varepsilon$ for high-DPRI datasets. We
do not evaluate DP-SGD in this work, so we state this only as a
hypothesis, motivated by the DPRI ranking and by the bound in
Theorem~\ref{thm:main}, which ties advantage to data geometry
independently of the privacy mechanism.

\subsection{Policy Implication}

Current regulatory frameworks for machine learning privacy treat privacy
largely as a property of the processing rather than of the data. GDPR's
data protection impact assessments (Article 35) ask what processing is
planned and what safeguards surround it; HIPAA's de-identification
standard asks whether identifiers have been removed or an expert deems
re-identification risk small. Neither asks the question our
measurements show matters at comparable magnitude: how exposed is this
particular dataset's structure once a model is trained on it? A
pre-training, data-level risk score gives these assessments a
quantitative input they currently lack. A DPIA for an ML system could
record the dataset's DPRI profile and justify the chosen safeguards
against it, and an expert-determination review could treat high sample
uniqueness the way it already treats quasi-identifiers, as structural
evidence of elevated risk. DPRI is one concrete and inexpensive
instance of such a score, and the cost asymmetry, minutes on a laptop
against the weeks of a full privacy evaluation, makes it deployable at
the point where release decisions are actually made.

\subsection{Extensions}

Two settings extend naturally beyond our centralized scope. In federated
learning, where unintended feature leakage is already
documented~\cite{melis2019exploiting}, each client holds a local dataset
with its own DPRI, and a high-DPRI client could be asked to contribute
more noise in the local differential-privacy step. Generative models are
the second: membership inference applies to them as
well~\cite{hayes2019logan,carlini2021extracting}, and because DPRI is
defined on the training data distribution rather than on any model, it
applies in principle to GANs, diffusion models, and language models. We
leave the empirical validation of both extensions to future work.
