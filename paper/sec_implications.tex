\section{Security Implications}
\label{sec:implications}
% =============================================================================

\subsection{Dual Use: Reconnaissance and Auditing}

DPRI can be computed in minutes from raw data, without training a model.
This makes it dual-use. An adversary with access to several candidate
datasets, for example a public benchmark suite or a leaked schema, can use
DPRI to decide which to attack, focusing effort on the highest-uniqueness,
highest-risk targets rather than attacking blindly. Because DPRI requires no
model and no queries, this reconnaissance is essentially free.

The same property serves defenders. A data holder can compute DPRI before
releasing a dataset or a model trained on it. A high score, driven by high
sample uniqueness or dimensionality, signals that the data is intrinsically
more exposed and should trigger stronger safeguards such as data
minimization, stricter access controls, or a tighter differential-privacy
budget. The asymmetry favors the defender, who computes DPRI once at
collection time, while the attacker still has to mount the actual attack.

\subsection{Toward Data-Aware Differential Privacy}

Our results suggest, but do not establish, that a fixed differential-privacy
budget protects different datasets unequally, because the underlying risk
differs before any noise is added. This is consistent with two known
observations: that the empirical protection delivered by a given
$\varepsilon$ varies widely in practice~\cite{jayaraman2019evaluating},
and that the cost of private learning depends strongly on how the data is
represented~\cite{tramer2021differentially}. A natural direction is a
data-aware budget that tightens $\varepsilon$ for high-DPRI datasets. We
do not evaluate DP-SGD in this work, so we state this only as a
hypothesis, motivated by the DPRI ranking and by the bound in
Theorem~\ref{thm:main}, which ties advantage to data geometry
independently of the privacy mechanism.

\subsection{Policy Implication}

Current regulatory frameworks for machine learning privacy, for example GDPR
Article 22 and HIPAA de-identification, treat privacy largely as a property
of the algorithm rather than the data. Our measurements indicate that the
data itself is a comparable driver of membership inference risk. This
suggests that data-release governance could benefit from a standardized,
pre-training, data-level risk assessment, of which DPRI is one concrete and
inexpensive instance.

\subsection{Extensions}

Two settings extend naturally beyond our centralized scope. In federated
learning, where unintended feature leakage is already
documented~\cite{melis2019exploiting}, each client holds a local dataset
with its own DPRI, and a high-DPRI client could be asked to contribute
more noise in the local differential-privacy step. Generative models are
the second: membership inference applies to them as
well~\cite{hayes2019logan,carlini2021extracting}, and because DPRI is
defined on the training data distribution rather than on any model, it
applies in principle to GANs, diffusion models, and language models. We
leave the empirical validation of both extensions to future work.
