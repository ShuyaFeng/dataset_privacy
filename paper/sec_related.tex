\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}
% =============================================================================

\paragraph{Membership inference attacks.}
Membership inference predates machine learning: Homer et
al.~\cite{homer2008resolving} resolved an individual's presence in
genomic aggregate statistics. Shokri et al.~\cite{shokri2017membership}
brought the attack to ML models, introduced the shadow-model technique,
and established Purchase100 and Texas100 as standard benchmarks.
Subsequent work strengthened attacks via relaxed shadow-model
assumptions~\cite{salem2019ml}, white-box
access~\cite{nasr2019comprehensive,leino2020stolen}, label-only
access~\cite{choquette2021label}, blind differential
comparison~\cite{hui2021blindmi}, per-sample difficulty
calibration~\cite{watson2022importance,carlini2022lira}, enhanced
hypothesis tests~\cite{ye2022enhanced}, and low-cost
reference-model designs~\cite{zarifzadeh2024rmia}; the likelihood-ratio
attack LiRA~\cite{carlini2022lira} serves as our strongest ground-truth
attack. Membership inference has also been demonstrated against
aggregate location data~\cite{pyrgelis2018knock}, collaborative
learning~\cite{melis2019exploiting}, and generative
models~\cite{hayes2019logan}. Song and
Mittal~\cite{song2021systematic} systematize the empirical evaluation of
these risks, and surveys organize the field~\cite{hu2022membership,papernot2018sok}.
Our work does not propose a new attack. Rather, it ranks datasets by
their attack success from data properties alone, before any model is
trained.

\paragraph{Defenses and their evaluation.}
Dedicated MIA defenses include adversarial
regularization~\cite{nasr2018machine}, inference-time output
perturbation~\cite{jia2019memguard}, and differentially private
training~\cite{abadi2016deep}. Aerni et al.~\cite{aerni2024evaluations}
show that defense evaluations are frequently misleading because of the
evaluation choices made, and Duan et al.~\cite{duan2024membership} find
that MIA results on large language models depend heavily on the
evaluated data. Both critiques are adjacent to our Findings~1 and~2:
the dataset is an unreported experimental factor large enough to change
conclusions.

\paragraph{Data uniqueness and re-identification.}
A long pre-ML literature ties privacy risk to intrinsic data structure.
Sweeney's $k$-anonymity~\cite{sweeney2002k} and its
refinements~\cite{machanavajjhala2007diversity,li2007tcloseness} measure
risk by record indistinguishability; Narayanan and
Shmatikov~\cite{narayanan2008robust} exploited the sparsity of the
Netflix ratings matrix for de-anonymization; de Montjoye et al.\ showed
that a handful of points make mobility traces~\cite{demontjoye2013unique}
and credit-card metadata~\cite{demontjoye2015unique} unique; and Rocher
et al.~\cite{rocher2019estimating} predict re-identification success
from generative models of the data. DPRI carries this lineage into
machine learning: where these works score the re-identifiability of a
\emph{released} dataset, we score the membership inference risk that a
dataset induces in \emph{models trained on it}, before training.

\paragraph{Privacy risk theory.}
Yeom et al.~\cite{yeom2018privacy} proved that MIA advantage is bounded
by the generalization gap, linking privacy to overfitting.
Sablayrolles et al.~\cite{sablayrolles2019whitebox} characterized the
Bayes-optimal MIA as a likelihood ratio test, which forms the basis of
our Theorem~\ref{thm:main}.
Our contribution extends these results by showing that the likelihood
ratio, and hence the MIA advantage, is bounded, under explicit modeling
assumptions, by pre-training distributional statistics, not only by
post-training model properties.

\paragraph{Memorization and long-tail data.}
Zhang et al.~\cite{zhang2017understanding} showed that deep networks can
memorize arbitrary labels; Carlini et al.~\cite{carlini2019secret}
showed that such memorization is extractable.
Feldman~\cite{feldman2020memorization} proved that learning algorithms
must memorize atypical (long-tail) training examples to achieve
near-optimal generalization, with theoretical
extensions~\cite{brown2021memorization} and empirical confirmation via
influence estimation~\cite{feldman2020neural} and counterfactual
memorization~\cite{zhang2021counterfactual}.
Carlini et al.~\cite{carlini2021extracting} showed that LLMs memorize
rare training examples verbatim, and the onion
effect~\cite{carlini2022onion} shows that removing the most vulnerable
outliers exposes the next layer, underscoring that vulnerability is a
property of the data distribution.
Our work differs along three dimensions.
Unit of analysis: we study dataset-level statistics rather than
per-sample certificates.
Timing: we operate before training, with no trained model.
Output: a dataset-level risk score used to rank datasets, not a post-hoc
per-sample memorization label.
Our results are consistent with this line. The datasets we find most at
risk are those with high sample uniqueness, the aggregate counterpart of
atypical, memorization-prone examples. We complement that work by
asking, at the dataset level and before training, how far this geometry
predicts risk, and we find that it does so moderately but significantly.

\paragraph{Differential privacy.}
DP-SGD~\cite{abadi2016deep} is the standard mechanism for training
private neural networks~\cite{dwork2014algorithmic}.
Empirical audits~\cite{jagielski2020auditing,nasr2021adversary,steinke2023privacy}
show that its guarantees are tight, Bayesian estimation recovers the
$\varepsilon$ actually achieved~\cite{zanella2023bayesian}, and
Jayaraman and Evans~\cite{jayaraman2019evaluating} document the gap
between nominal budgets and practical protection.
None of these works ask how the benefit of a given $\varepsilon$
varies with dataset structure. We raise this as a direction motivated by
our bound (Section~\ref{sec:implications}), though we do not evaluate
DP-SGD in this work.

\paragraph{Dataset auditing and data valuation.}
Long et al.~\cite{long2020pragmatic} propose a pragmatic approach to
membership inference that accounts for dataset characteristics, and ML
Privacy Meter~\cite{murakonda2020ml} quantifies model privacy risk for
regulatory compliance, but both require trained (target or reference)
models. Data valuation methods such as influence
functions~\cite{koh2017understanding} and Data
Shapley~\cite{ghorbani2019data} likewise score data through a model.
Our work differs in requiring no model at all: DPRI predicts risk before
training and provides a formal basis through Theorem~\ref{thm:main}.
