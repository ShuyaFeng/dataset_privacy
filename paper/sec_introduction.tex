% =============================================================================
\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
% =============================================================================

Over the past decade, membership inference attacks
(MIA)~\cite{homer2008resolving,shokri2017membership} have become the
standard tool for measuring how much a trained model leaks about its
training data. Hundreds of papers have proposed stronger
attacks~\cite{salem2019ml,nasr2019comprehensive,choquette2021label,song2021systematic,carlini2022lira,ye2022enhanced,watson2022importance,zarifzadeh2024rmia},
dedicated defenses~\cite{nasr2018machine,jia2019memguard,abadi2016deep},
tighter theoretical
bounds~\cite{yeom2018privacy,sablayrolles2019whitebox}, and
systematizations of the resulting
landscape~\cite{papernot2018sok,hu2022membership}. The same
model-centric view organizes the sibling lines of work: attribute and
property inference~\cite{fredrikson2015model,ganju2018property},
training-data
extraction~\cite{carlini2019secret,carlini2021extracting}, differential
privacy~\cite{dwork2006calibrating,abadi2016deep}, and machine
unlearning~\cite{cao2015towards,bourtoule2021machine,chen2021unlearning}
all quantify, bound, or remove leakage from a trained model. Yet a more
fundamental question has gone largely unanswered:

\smallskip
\begin{center}
Why do some datasets leak more than others,\\
independent of the model trained on them?
\end{center}
\smallskip

The question is not only an engineering one. Long before machine
learning, the privacy literature understood that re-identification risk
lives in the data itself: in the sparsity of the Netflix ratings
matrix~\cite{narayanan2008robust}, in the uniqueness of mobility
traces~\cite{demontjoye2013unique} and credit-card
metadata~\cite{demontjoye2015unique}, and in the incompleteness that
defeats naive anonymization~\cite{sweeney2002k,rocher2019estimating}.
Machine learning privacy has largely set this lesson aside. The
prevailing experimental template picks a dataset, trains a model,
reports an AUC, and attributes the number to the attack or the defense,
with the dataset as inert backdrop.

Our measurements say otherwise. When we decompose the variation in
attack AUC across 31 datasets, three attacks, and three model families,
the dataset emerges as a first-class experimental factor: among the
regularized models that practitioners actually deploy, dataset choice
explains $49.5\%$ of the variation, twice the share of model
architecture ($24.4\%$) and well above the attack method ($26.1\%$).
Only a deliberately overfit model, an unbounded-depth Random Forest that
memorizes its training set outright, erases this margin
(Section~\ref{sec:finding1}). Benchmark practice compounds the problem:
Purchase100 and Texas100~\cite{shokri2017membership}, the two most
common MIA benchmarks, turn out to be geometric outliers whose sample
uniqueness is the highest among all 31 datasets we study, so results
reported only on them measure privacy risk in an unrepresentatively
high-risk regime.

These observations motivate a temporally prior question: can the privacy
risk of a dataset be anticipated before any model is trained? We give a
qualified yes. We introduce the Dataset Privacy Risk Index (DPRI), a
scalar risk estimator computed entirely from distributional statistics
of the raw data: sample uniqueness, local density, outlier structure,
feature entropy, class separability, and dimensionality. Each statistic
is motivated by a theoretical result (Theorem~\ref{thm:main}) showing
that the advantage of a Bayes-optimal membership adversary is bounded by
a function of local data geometry. Across the 31 datasets, DPRI ranks
risk with a Spearman correlation of $0.61$ ($p<0.001$) under a nested
cross-validation that selects features on training folds only, an
estimate free of the selection optimism that inflates small-sample
results; the same discipline exposed our own early estimates, on fewer
datasets, as artifacts (Section~\ref{sec:power}). Data geometry thus
significantly shapes privacy risk without fully determining it. We treat
this calibrated, qualified finding as a result in its own right: it
bounds what any pre-training predictor can achieve.

Our question is related to, but distinct from, memorization theory.
Feldman~\cite{feldman2020memorization} showed that rare individual
samples must be memorized for good generalization, a phenomenon
observable only after training. Where that line of work identifies which
samples a given model will memorize, we predict how much a dataset will
leak, before training and at the dataset level, when a practitioner can
still act on the answer.

In summary, this paper makes the following contributions:

\begin{itemize}

\item The first formal bound linking the advantage of an optimal
membership inference adversary to pre-training data geometry, namely
sample uniqueness and local density (Theorem~\ref{thm:main}), whose
predicted scaling law holds almost exactly on synthetic data.

\item The Dataset Privacy Risk Index, six distributional features that
rank the MIA risk of 31 datasets across five domains at Spearman $0.61$
under unbiased nested cross-validation.

\item Evidence that among realistic models, dataset structure governs
MIA risk more than model architecture and far more than the choice of
attack, so single-benchmark attack numbers conflate three sources of
variation.

\item Evidence that the benchmarks anchoring a decade of MIA research
are geometric outliers, an effect Corollary~\ref{cor:benchmark} predicts
from how those benchmarks were constructed.

\item An open-source auditor that scores any tabular dataset in minutes
on a laptop, with no model training and no GPU.

\end{itemize}

We study 31 public datasets spanning tabular, image, network, medical,
and recommendation domains, in the centralized training setting;
extensions to federated learning and generative models are discussed as
future work in Section~\ref{sec:implications}.
