\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
% =============================================================================

Over the past decade, membership inference attacks (MIA)~\cite{shokri2017membership}
have become the standard tool for measuring how much a trained model leaks
about its training data.
Hundreds of papers have proposed stronger attacks~\cite{carlini2022lira,ye2022enhanced},
better defenses~\cite{nasr2019comprehensive}, and tighter theoretical
bounds~\cite{yeom2018privacy,sablayrolles2019whitebox}.
Yet a more fundamental question has gone largely unanswered:

\smallskip
\begin{center}
\emph{Why do some datasets leak more than others,\\
independent of the model trained on them?}
\end{center}
\smallskip

This is not an engineering question.
It is a question about the nature of data itself.
If membership inference is the symptom, then data geometry is the disease.

\paragraph{The problem with current practice.}
Every MIA paper chooses a dataset, trains a model, and reports an AUC.
The AUC is then attributed to the attack method or the defense mechanism.
But our work shows empirically that dataset choice explains
\TODO{XX}\% of AUC variance, while model architecture explains only
\TODO{XX}\%.
The field has been optimizing the wrong variable.

A second pathology is benchmark reliance.
Purchase100 and Texas100~\cite{shokri2017membership} are the two most
common MIA benchmarks.
We show that both datasets are structural outliers: their data geometry
places them in the top \TODO{X}\% of DPRI scores across all datasets we
studied, meaning papers that report results only on these benchmarks are
measuring privacy risk in an unrepresentatively high-risk regime.

\paragraph{Our approach.}
We ask a temporally prior question:
\emph{Can the privacy risk of a dataset be reliably predicted before any
model is trained?}
If the answer is yes, practitioners gain an actionable tool: compute the
risk score at data collection time, before committing to a training
pipeline or a privacy budget.

We answer yes, and we formalize this through the
\emph{Dataset Privacy Risk Index} (DPRI)---a scalar pre-training
risk estimator derived from five distributional statistics:
sample uniqueness, local density, outlier score, feature entropy,
and class cluster separation.
Each statistic is motivated by a theoretical result
(Theorem~\ref{thm:main}) showing that MIA advantage is bounded by a
function of local data geometry.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{itemize}

\item \textbf{Theoretical foundation.}
We prove that the Bayes-optimal MIA advantage for a sample $x$ is
upper-bounded by $O(u(x) / \rho(x)^{1/d})$, where $u(x)$ is sample
uniqueness and $\rho(x)$ is local density---both computable from raw data
(Theorem~\ref{thm:main}).
This provides the first formal link between pre-training data geometry
and membership inference risk.

\item \textbf{Three security findings.}
(F1)~Dataset structure is the dominant driver of MIA risk; model
architecture contributes a secondary, bounded effect.
(F2)~Widely used MIA benchmarks are structural outliers that inflate
reported attack success by \TODO{X.X}$\times$, casting doubt on the

(F3)~DP protection efficacy is a function of DPRI, not of $\varepsilon$

\item \textbf{Open-source tool.}
We release DPRI as a Python package that audits any tabular dataset in
under two minutes on a laptop, outputting a risk score and
DP budget recommendation.

\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Scope.}
We focus on tabular and graph-structured datasets in the centralized
training setting.
Extensions to image data, federated learning, and generative models are
left as future work.
All datasets used are publicly available; no proprietary data was accessed.
