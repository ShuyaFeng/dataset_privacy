% =============================================================================
\section{Dataset Privacy Risk Index}
\label{sec:dpri}
% =============================================================================

Motivated by Theorem~\ref{thm:main}, we define five distributional
features that are computable from raw data before any model training.
Each feature targets a geometric property that the theorem identifies as
a driver of MIA advantage.

\subsection{Feature Definitions}

Let $D = \{x_1,\dots,x_n\}$ denote the feature matrix (labels used only
for Feature~5).
Per-sample scores (Features~1--3) are aggregated to dataset level by their
mean; together with the two dataset-level scalars (Features~4--5) and the
data dimensionality, they form a six-dimensional descriptor
$\phi(D) \in \mathbb{R}^{6}$ (Section~\ref{sec:aggregation}).

\paragraph{Feature 1: Sample Uniqueness $u(x)$.}
\begin{equation}
  u(x) = \min_{x' \in D,\, x'\neq x} \|x - x'\|_2
\end{equation}
High $u(x)$ means $x$ is isolated from all other training points.
Isolated samples have high MIA advantage by Equation~\eqref{eq:bound}.
In practice we estimate $u(x)$ by the distance to the 5th nearest
neighbour rather than the literal minimum above, since the nearest
neighbour alone ($k=1$) is noisy for dense datasets.

\paragraph{Feature 2: Local Density $\rho(x)$.}
\begin{equation}
  \rho(x) = \frac{1}{r_k(x)}, \quad
  r_k(x) = \text{dist.\ to $k$-th nearest neighbour}
\end{equation}
This is a monotone surrogate of the $k$-NN density estimate in
Equation~\eqref{eq:density}; it preserves the ranking the theory needs
while avoiding high-dimensional ball volumes, which underflow numerically
for large $d$.
Low $\rho(x)$ (sparse region) implies large $u(x)$ and high risk.
The two features are theoretically related but empirically complementary:
$u(x)$ captures global isolation while $\rho(x)$ captures local structure.

\paragraph{Feature 3: Outlier Score $o(x)$.}
\begin{equation}
  o(x) = \frac{1}{2}\bigl(\mathrm{LOF}_{\mathrm{norm}}(x)
                         + \mathrm{iForest}_{\mathrm{norm}}(x)\bigr)
\end{equation}
An ensemble of Local Outlier Factor~\cite{breunig2000lof} and Isolation
Forest~\cite{liu2008isolation}, each normalized to $[0,1]$.
The ensemble reduces the sensitivity of any single outlier detector to
its specific density assumption.

\paragraph{Feature 4: Feature Entropy $H(X)$.}
\begin{equation}
  H(X) = \frac{1}{d} \sum_{j=1}^{d} H(X_j), \quad
  H(X_j) = -\sum_b p_b \log_2 p_b
\end{equation}
The average Shannon entropy~\cite{shannon1948mathematical} across all
features $X_j$, computed over a histogram discretization.
High-entropy features increase the dimensionality of the space in which
samples are embedded, reducing density and increasing uniqueness.

\paragraph{Feature 5: Cluster Separation $S$.}
The silhouette score~\cite{rousseeuw1987silhouettes} on class labels:
\begin{equation}
  S = \mathrm{Silhouette}(X, y)
  = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} \frac{b(x_i) - a(x_i)}{\max(a(x_i), b(x_i))}
\end{equation}
where $a(x_i)$ is the mean intra-class distance and $b(x_i)$ is the
mean nearest-class distance.
Well-separated classes ($S$ near $1$) place decision boundary samples
in high-contrast regions, increasing their MIA risk.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{DPRI feature extraction}
\label{alg:dpri}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require standardized feature matrix $X \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times d}$, labels $y$
\Ensure descriptor $\phi(D) \in \mathbb{R}^{6}$
\State build a $k$-NN index on $X$ \hfill ($k = 5$)
\For{$i = 1, \dots, n$}
  \State $u_i \gets$ distance from $x_i$ to its $k$-th nearest neighbour
  \State $\rho_i \gets 1 / \max\bigl(r_k(x_i),\ \epsilon_D\bigr)$
         \hfill (floor $\epsilon_D$: Sec.~\ref{sec:design})
  \State $o_i \gets \tfrac{1}{2}\bigl(\mathrm{LOF}_{\mathrm{norm}}(x_i)
         + \mathrm{iForest}_{\mathrm{norm}}(x_i)\bigr)$
\EndFor
\State $H \gets \frac{1}{d}\sum_{j=1}^{d}$ entropy of a 50-bin histogram
       of feature $j$
\State $S \gets$ silhouette$(X, y)$
       \hfill (5{,}000-point subsample if $n > 5{,}000$)
\State \Return $\phi(D) = (\bar{u},\ \bar{\rho},\ \bar{o},\ H,\ S,\ \log d)$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Aggregation and Risk Prediction}
\label{sec:aggregation}

Per-sample scores ($u$, $\rho$, $o$) are aggregated to the dataset level
by their mean. The choice is not arbitrary: the dataset-level bound in
Equation~\eqref{eq:risk_bound} is itself an average of per-sample
geometric factors, so mean aggregation is the statistic the theorem
directly speaks about. To these five geometric statistics we append the
data dimensionality $\log d$, which captures that high-dimensional data
is sparser and therefore more memorizable, giving a six-dimensional
descriptor $\phi(D)$. Algorithm~\ref{alg:dpri} summarizes the full
extraction.

To predict $\mathrm{Risk}(D)$ from $\phi(D)$ we use ridge regression with two
deliberate choices. First, the geometric features are heavily right-skewed
(local density spans two orders of magnitude), so we rank-transform each
feature within cross-validation folds. Second, because $n=31$ is small
relative to the feature count, we select the feature subset by nested
cross-validation rather than fitting one fixed high-dimensional model.
Section~\ref{sec:results} reports the resulting unbiased rank correlation.
We emphasize ranking over absolute prediction. A pre-training index needs to
order datasets by risk, not to estimate an exact AUC.

\subsection{Design Choices and Robustness}
\label{sec:design}

Real datasets stress geometric statistics in ways that uniform theory
does not, and three implementation choices matter in practice. First,
the density surrogate $1/r_k(x)$ diverges on duplicated rows, which are
common in categorical data: COMPAS and Gowalla both contain many exact
repeats, for which $r_k(x) \approx 0$. We therefore floor $r_k$ at
$10\%$ of its median over the dataset, which keeps duplicates ``very
dense'' without letting a handful of repeated rows dominate the dataset
mean. Second, the outlier ensemble combines two detectors with
complementary failure modes: LOF (20 neighbours) is local and misses
globally displaced clusters, while Isolation Forest (200 trees) is
global and misses locally anomalous points inside dense regions;
each score is min-max normalized to $[0,1]$ before averaging so that
neither scale dominates. Third, feature entropy uses a fixed 50-bin
histogram per feature, which is coarse enough to be stable at
$n$ of a few hundred yet fine enough to separate near-constant from
high-variability features.

The regression stage is deliberately conservative. With 31 observations
and six collinear features, a flexible model memorizes the sample;
Section~\ref{sec:results} shows Random Forest regression doing exactly
that. Ridge regression on rank-transformed features, with the feature
subset chosen by an inner cross-validation loop that never sees the
held-out dataset, is the least expressive estimator that still captures
the monotone relationships our theory predicts, and the nested protocol
guarantees that the reported correlation contains no selection
optimism.

\subsection{Computation Cost}

All features require only standard nearest-neighbour and density-estimation
routines. On a commodity laptop DPRI computation takes seconds for
datasets up to roughly ten thousand samples, and a few minutes for the
largest datasets we study (Purchase100 at 197k samples, Texas100 at 6169
features), dominated by the nearest-neighbour search.
No GPU is required.
This is the key practical advantage over MIA-based risk assessment, which
requires training and attacking many models.
