\section{Background and Threat Model}
\label{sec:background}

\subsection{Membership Inference Attacks}

Let $D = \{(x_i, y_i)\}_{i=1}^{n}$ be a training dataset drawn i.i.d.\
from an unknown distribution $\mathcal{P}$ over $\mathcal{X} \times \mathcal{Y}$.
A learner trains a classifier $f_\theta : \mathcal{X} \to \Delta(\mathcal{Y})$
by minimizing empirical risk on $D$.
A membership inference attack (MIA) is an algorithm
$\mathcal{A}$ that, given a target sample $(x, y)$ and query access to
$f_\theta$, outputs $1$ if $(x,y) \in D$ and $0$ otherwise.

The MIA advantage of $\mathcal{A}$ is:
\begin{multline}
  \mathrm{Adv}(\mathcal{A}, f_\theta, D)
  =
  \bigl|
    \Pr[\mathcal{A}=1 \mid (x,y)\in D] \\
    -
    \Pr[\mathcal{A}=1 \mid (x,y)\notin D]
  \bigr|.
  \label{eq:adv}
\end{multline}
We denote the dataset-level risk as the expected advantage over samples:
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{Risk}(D) = \sup_{\mathcal{A}} \;\mathbb{E}_{(x,y)\sim\mathcal{P}}\,
  \mathrm{Adv}(\mathcal{A}, f_\theta, D).
  \label{eq:risk}
\end{equation}
The supremum in Equation~\eqref{eq:risk} is not directly observable.
In practice we estimate $\mathrm{Risk}(D)$ by the mean AUC over a grid of
strong attacks and model families, a typical-adversary surrogate;
Section~\ref{sec:setup} defines the grid and explains why the mean,
rather than the maximum, is the informative estimator.

\subsection{Differential Privacy}

An algorithm $\mathcal{M}$ is $(\varepsilon,\delta)$-differentially
private~\cite{dwork2006calibrating,dwork2014algorithmic} if for all
adjacent datasets $D, D'$
differing in one record and all outputs $S$:
$\Pr[\mathcal{M}(D)\in S] \leq e^\varepsilon \Pr[\mathcal{M}(D')\in S] + \delta$.
Yeom et al.~\cite{yeom2018privacy} showed that
$\mathrm{Adv}(\mathcal{A}^*) \leq e^\varepsilon - 1 + \delta$
for $(\varepsilon,\delta)$-DP mechanisms.
This worst-case bound does not capture how the practical risk varies with
data geometry across datasets, a question we take up in
Section~\ref{sec:implications}.

\subsection{Threat Model}
\label{sec:threat}

We consider two principals: a data holder who trains a model on $D$ and
may later publish it, and an adversary who, given a target sample
$(x,y)$ and oracle access to $f_\theta$, seeks to determine whether
$(x,y) \in D$. The adversary operates in the black-box setting: it
submits inputs and observes predicted class probabilities, but cannot
inspect model weights. This is the standard setting for deployed APIs
and MLaaS platforms~\cite{shokri2017membership,truex2019demystifying}
and the weakest, hence most broadly applicable, adversary
model~\cite{salem2019ml}; white-box access only strengthens the attacks
we consider~\cite{nasr2019comprehensive,leino2020stolen}. We assume the
adversary knows the data domain distribution $\mathcal{P}$, for example
that the dataset contains medical records of a specific demographic, but
not the exact set $D$.

The defender's goal is to compute $\mathrm{DPRI}(D)$ before training,
from raw data alone and with no model required, in order to estimate
$\mathrm{Risk}(D)$ and to inform the choice of differential-privacy
budget and the release decision. The same capability cuts the other way:
an adversary with access to several candidate target datasets can
compute DPRI pre-attack and prioritize the high-risk ones, maximizing
attack return on a fixed query budget. We analyze this reconnaissance
threat and its mitigations in Section~\ref{sec:implications}.

Throughout, we assume centralized training and a passive adversary (no
data poisoning); extensions to federated learning are discussed in
Section~\ref{sec:implications}.
