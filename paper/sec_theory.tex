\section{Theoretical Analysis}
\label{sec:theory}

We establish that MIA advantage is upper-bounded by data-distributional
quantities computable before training.
All proofs are in Appendix~\ref{app:proofs}.

\subsection{Notation}

Let $D = \{x_1,\dots,x_n\} \subset \mathcal{X}$ be drawn i.i.d.\ from
density $p$ on $\mathcal{X} \subseteq \mathbb{R}^d$.
For any $x \in D$, define:
\begin{align}
  u(x) &= \min_{x' \in D,\, x'\neq x} \|x - x'\|_2,
           \label{eq:uniqueness}\\
  \rho(x) &= \frac{k}{ n \cdot \mathrm{Vol}(B(x,\, r_k(x)))},
           \label{eq:density}
\end{align}
the sample uniqueness (nearest-neighbour distance) and the local density
($k$-NN estimate), where $r_k(x)$ is the distance to the $k$-th nearest
neighbour of $x$ in $D \setminus \{x\}$ and $\mathrm{Vol}(\cdot)$ denotes
Euclidean ball volume.

For the in-distribution and out-distribution of a trained model
$f_\theta$, define the score distributions:
\begin{align}
  P_{\mathrm{in}}(s) &= \Pr[\ell(f_\theta, x) \leq s \mid x \in D],\\
  P_{\mathrm{out}}(s) &= \Pr[\ell(f_\theta, x) \leq s \mid x \notin D],
\end{align}
where $\ell(f_\theta,x)$ is the model's loss on sample $x$.

\subsection{Main Theorem}

\begin{theorem}[DPRI Upper Bound on MIA Advantage]
\label{thm:main}
Let $f_\theta$ be trained on $D$ by minimising empirical risk.
Assume the loss function $\ell$ is $L$-Lipschitz in $x$ and that the
non-member loss distribution $P_{\mathrm{out}}$ is sub-Gaussian with
variance proxy $\sigma^2$.
Then for any sample $x \in D$:
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{Adv}(\mathcal{A}^*, f_\theta, x)
  \;\leq\;
  \sqrt{\frac{L^2 \cdot u(x)^2}{2\,\sigma^2}}
  \;\cdot\;
  \frac{1}{\rho(x)^{1/d}},
  \label{eq:bound}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{A}^*$ is the Bayes-optimal adversary,
$\sigma^2$ is the variance of the loss under $P_{\mathrm{out}}$,
and $d$ is the data dimensionality.

At the dataset level, the aggregate advantage satisfies:
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{Risk}(D)
  \;\leq\;
  \frac{1}{n}\sum_{x \in D}
  \sqrt{\frac{L^2 \cdot u(x)^2}{2\,\sigma^2\,\rho(x)^{2/d}}}.
  \label{eq:risk_bound}
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

The bound reads naturally: privacy risk increases when a sample is far
from its neighbours ($u(x)$ large, i.e.\ isolated) and when the local
population is sparse ($\rho(x)$ small). Both quantities are computable
directly from $D$, with no model training, while the Lipschitz constant
$L$ and noise level $\sigma$ are model-family constants that can be
estimated or treated as fixed hyperparameters. We state
Theorem~\ref{thm:main} as a modeling result under explicit, standard
distributional assumptions (made precise in Appendix~\ref{app:proofs}),
not as a worst-case guarantee. Its value lies in the falsifiable
qualitative prediction it makes, that risk rises with uniqueness and
falls with density, which Section~\ref{sec:results} tests directly
against measured attack AUC.

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
We proceed in three steps.

Step 1: KL to advantage.
By Pinsker's inequality~\cite{tsybakov2009introduction}, the Bayes-optimal
MIA advantage is:
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{Adv}(\mathcal{A}^*) \leq
  \sqrt{\tfrac{1}{2}\,\mathrm{KL}(P_{\mathrm{in}} \| P_{\mathrm{out}})}.
  \label{eq:bh}
\end{equation}

Step 2: KL in terms of loss gap.
Since $P_{\mathrm{in}}$ and $P_{\mathrm{out}}$ are loss score distributions,
and $\ell$ is $L$-Lipschitz, the expected loss gap between a training
sample $x$ and the nearest out-of-sample point $x'$ satisfies:
\begin{equation}
  \mathbb{E}[\ell(f_\theta, x)] - \mathbb{E}[\ell(f_\theta, x')]
  \;\leq\; L \cdot \|x - x'\|_2
  \;\leq\; L \cdot u(x).
  \label{eq:lipschitz_gap}
\end{equation}
Under a sub-Gaussian model for $P_{\mathrm{out}}$ with variance $\sigma^2$:
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{KL}(P_{\mathrm{in}} \| P_{\mathrm{out}})
  \;\leq\;
  \frac{(L \cdot u(x))^2}{2\,\sigma^2}.
  \label{eq:kl_bound}
\end{equation}

Step 3: Density correction.
The nearest-neighbour distance $u(x)$ and local density $\rho(x)$ are
related by the $k$-NN geometry: for a sample in a region of density
$\rho$, the expected distance to the nearest neighbour scales as
$\rho(x)^{-1/d}$ (standard result, see \cite{biau2015lectures}).
Substituting into~\eqref{eq:kl_bound} and applying~\eqref{eq:bh}
yields~\eqref{eq:bound}.
Averaging over all $x \in D$ gives~\eqref{eq:risk_bound}.
\qed
\end{proof}

\subsection{Assumptions and Their Plausibility}
\label{sec:assumptions}

The proof rests on three modeling assumptions, stated formally in
Appendix~\ref{app:proofs}; here we discuss when each holds and when it
fails, because the failure modes are themselves informative.

The first assumption (A1) models the member and non-member loss-score
distributions as Gaussian with common variance. For regularized models,
whose loss scores aggregate many weakly dependent contributions, the
score distributions are unimodal and the approximation is reasonable;
it is the same assumption under which the loss-threshold attack is
Bayes-optimal~\cite{sablayrolles2019whitebox,carlini2022lira}. The
assumption breaks for a model that interpolates its training set: an
unbounded-depth Random Forest drives member losses to near zero,
producing a degenerate, sharply bimodal score distribution. This is
precisely the model that dominates our grid as an adversarial worst
case (Section~\ref{sec:finding1}), so the theory predicts its own
boundary of applicability.

The second assumption (A2), Lipschitz continuity of the loss in the
input, holds for the cross-entropy of a softmax network with bounded
weights on a bounded domain; our preprocessing standardizes every
feature (Section~\ref{sec:setup}), keeping inputs bounded in practice.
The third (A3), the classical relation $r_k(x) \asymp \rho(x)^{-1/d}$
between nearest-neighbour distance and local
density~\cite{biau2015lectures}, requires the density to be
approximately constant over the $k$-NN neighbourhood and degrades near
manifold boundaries, one reason we expect, and observe, a moderate
rather than perfect correlation on real data.

Equally important is what the theorem does not claim. It is not a
per-sample certificate, not a worst-case guarantee over all training
algorithms, and not a substitute for differential privacy. It is a
falsifiable statement about which distributional quantities govern
membership leakage, and Sections~\ref{sec:results}
and~\ref{sec:tightness_main} test it.

\subsection{Discussion of the Bound}

How tight is the bound, and what does it leave out? For datasets drawn
uniformly from a compact manifold it is tight up to constants
(Proposition~\ref{prop:tight}, Appendix~\ref{app:proofs}); for
real-world datasets it overestimates risk by a factor that shrinks as
the distribution becomes more regular. Looseness, however, does not
preclude predictive value. The geometric quantities the bound identifies
are each individually significant predictors of observed AUC (standalone
Spearman $|\rho|$ of $0.51$ to $0.57$, all $p<0.005$), and the combined
index ranks datasets at $\rho = 0.61$ under unbiased cross-validation
(Section~\ref{sec:results}). A synthetic experiment on uniform hypercube
data validates Proposition~\ref{prop:tight} directly: the bound factor
decays with fitted log-log slopes within $0.03$ of the predicted $-1/d$
for $d \in \{2,4,8\}$, and it ranks the measured attack advantage across
all $(n,d)$ configurations with Spearman $\rho = 0.89$
(Section~\ref{sec:tightness_main}, Figure~\ref{fig:tightness}).

A worked comparison makes the bound concrete. Texas100, the riskiest
dataset in our corpus, has mean uniqueness $\bar{u} = 55.7$ and mean
density $\bar{\rho} = 0.03$ in $d = 6{,}169$ dimensions
(Table~\ref{tab:dpri_features}); at that dimensionality the density
correction $\rho^{1/d}$ is essentially $1$, so the geometric factor is
dominated by uniqueness and sits near $56$. Gowalla, the least risky,
has $\bar{u} = 0.1$ and $\bar{\rho} = 34.9$ in $d = 6$ dimensions,
giving $\rho^{1/d} \approx 1.8$ and a geometric factor near $0.06$,
three orders of magnitude smaller. The measured risks, $0.92$ against
$0.51$, preserve exactly this ordering. The bound's absolute scale is
loose, as upper bounds tend to be, but the contrast it draws between a
sparse, high-dimensional record collection and a dense, low-dimensional
one is the contrast the attacks actually realize.

The bound also makes explicit what data alone cannot determine. It
factors into a data-geometry term, which varies widely across datasets,
and the model-family constants $L$ and $\sigma$, which contribute a
bounded multiplicative factor. This separation anticipates our empirical
decomposition, in which dataset structure and model architecture are
both substantial drivers of risk (Section~\ref{sec:finding1}). Finally,
the bound composes with differential privacy: if the training mechanism
is $(\epsilon,\delta)$-DP, then
$\mathrm{Adv}(\mathcal{A}^*) \leq e^\epsilon - 1 + \delta$~\cite{yeom2018privacy},
so a high-DPRI dataset requires a smaller $\epsilon$ to reach the same
residual risk. This motivates the data-aware DP direction we discuss in
Section~\ref{sec:implications}.

\subsection{Corollary: Benchmark Bias is Predictable}

\begin{corollary}[Benchmark datasets have structurally elevated DPRI]
\label{cor:benchmark}
If a benchmark dataset $D_b$ was constructed by subsampling or clustering
a larger dataset to create balanced, well-separated classes, then
$\mathrm{DPRI}(D_b) > \mathrm{DPRI}(D_{\mathrm{real}})$
for a real-world dataset $D_{\mathrm{real}}$ drawn from the same domain,
in expectation over the construction procedure.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
Subsampling increases $u(x)$ (nearest neighbours are removed) and
clustering increases class separation, both of which increase the RHS
of~\eqref{eq:risk_bound}.
\end{proof}

This corollary provides the theoretical underpinning for our empirical
Finding~2 (benchmark bias, Section~\ref{sec:finding2}).
