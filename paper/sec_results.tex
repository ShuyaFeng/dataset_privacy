% =============================================================================
\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}
% =============================================================================

We evaluate on 31 datasets across five domains, each attacked with three MIA
methods (LiRA, shadow, loss-threshold) under three model families (MLP,
XGBoost, Random Forest), for 279 (dataset, attack, model) configurations.

\subsection{Experimental Ground Truth}

We define the ground-truth risk of a dataset as the mean MIA AUC over the
nine attack-model configurations, $\mathrm{Risk}(D)$.
We use the mean rather than the per-dataset maximum because the maximum
collapses onto the Random Forest for every dataset. Random Forest with no
depth limit memorizes its training set, so its AUC saturates near $1.0$ and
erases all contrast between datasets. The mean preserves that contrast.

Aggregated over the grid (Table~\ref{tab:marginals}), LiRA is the
strongest attack (mean AUC $0.71$) ahead of loss-threshold and shadow
($0.61$ each), and Random Forest is the most vulnerable model (mean AUC
$0.75$) ahead of XGBoost ($0.62$) and MLP ($0.56$). Both orderings match
expectation: LiRA's per-sample calibration is what makes it the
state of the art, and the unregularized Random Forest is the only model
in the grid that interpolates its training set. More striking is the
third margin: $\mathrm{Risk}(D)$ ranges almost two-fold across datasets,
from $0.92$ (Texas100) down to $0.51$ (Gowalla, Mushroom). The choice of
dataset alone moves privacy risk by more than swapping either the attack
or the model.

\subsection{DPRI Ranks Privacy Risk}

We predict $\mathrm{Risk}(D)$ from the DPRI features using ridge regression
under leave-one-dataset-out cross-validation. Because the geometric features
are heavily right-skewed (local density spans $0.03$ to $35$), we
rank-transform features within each fold and report Spearman rank
correlation, which is the relevant metric for a pre-training risk index that
only needs to order datasets.

DPRI ranks datasets by risk with a Spearman correlation of $0.61$ under an
unbiased nested cross-validation that selects the feature subset on training
folds only (Table~\ref{tab:regression}). The fixed-feature LOO estimate is
$0.52$ with all features and $0.61$ with the geometric-core-plus-dimension
subset. Random Forest regression does worse ($\rho=0.36$), overfitting the
small sample. The prediction is moderate, not precise. Coefficients of
determination are not an informative lens here, since the target is a
ranking and the features are rank-transformed.
To our knowledge this is the first quantified estimate of how far
pre-training statistics alone can rank membership inference risk. Read as
a triage signal, $\rho=0.61$ is operationally useful: it separates the
high-risk tail from the low-risk bulk at zero training cost. Read as a
calibration, it sets the bar that any future pre-training risk predictor
must clear under equally strict validation.

\subsection{Which Factors Carry the Signal}
\label{sec:single_factor}

We assess each feature by its standalone Spearman correlation with
$\mathrm{Risk}(D)$ (Table~\ref{tab:single_factor}). A drop-one ablation is
not meaningful here, because the features are collinear and the linear
baseline is weak, so removing one feature lets a correlated feature absorb
its signal. Standalone rank correlation is robust to both.

Three geometric features are individually significant: local density,
class separability, and sample uniqueness (all $p < 0.005$). Outlier score,
feature entropy, and dimensionality are not individually significant at
$n=31$, though Random Forest assigns them nonzero importance, suggesting
they contribute weak or nonlinear signal. The headline result is that
no single statistic determines risk, and that the significant factors are
precisely the geometric ones our theory predicts.

\subsection{Where the Index Fails}
\label{sec:failures}

The exceptions in Table~\ref{tab:dpri_features} are not noise; they mark
the boundary of what geometry alone can know, and both have instructive
explanations. Creditg has the third-highest sample uniqueness
($\bar{u}=6.9$) yet only moderate risk ($0.77$). With $n=1{,}000$
samples in 74 dimensions, its uniqueness is inflated by sample size
rather than by genuinely atypical records: in so small a sample,
\emph{every} point is far from its neighbours, but the underlying
population is not unusually exposed. Uniqueness conflates ``rare in the
population'' with ``rare in this sample,'' and at small $n$ the two
diverge.

Mushroom fails in the opposite direction: moderate uniqueness
($\bar{u}=3.8$, well above Adult or Magic) yet the lowest risk in the
corpus ($0.51$). The dataset is famously perfectly separable, so every
model in our grid generalizes essentially without error, the
member and non-member loss distributions coincide, and there is no
membership signal for any attack to find, regardless of geometry. This
is the case our theory anticipates but cannot price: the bound's
geometric factor is necessary for leakage, not sufficient, because
leakage also requires the learning problem to be hard enough that the
model fits members more closely than non-members. A label-complexity
term would close this gap, but every candidate we know of requires
training a model, which is exactly what a pre-training index must avoid.
These two failure modes, small-sample uniqueness inflation and
perfectly learnable tasks, bound the achievable accuracy of any
geometry-only predictor and explain why we report $\rho = 0.61$ rather
than something closer to $1$.

\subsection{The Bound's Scaling Law on Synthetic Data}
\label{sec:tightness_main}

Before the two headline findings, we close the loop on the theory.
Proposition~\ref{prop:tight} makes a concrete, testable claim: for data
uniform on a $d$-dimensional hypercube, the bound's geometric factor
should decay as $n^{-1/d}$. We test it directly, drawing
$X \sim U[0,1]^d$ for $d \in \{2,4,8\}$ and $n$ from $250$ to $4{,}000$,
assigning binary labels by a fixed random hyperplane with $10\%$ label
noise so the model has something to memorize, training the MLP family
from our main grid, and measuring the loss-threshold attack advantage,
with five seeds per cell.

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \dprifig{figures/tightness_plot.pdf}{\linewidth}
  \caption{Scaling-law validation on uniform hypercube data (log-log).
           Left: the bound's geometric factor $\bar{u}$ decays with
           fitted slopes $-0.515$, $-0.277$, $-0.149$ for $d=2,4,8$,
           matching the predicted $-1/d$ rates of $-0.500$, $-0.250$,
           $-0.125$ within $0.03$. Right: measured advantage of the
           loss-threshold attack on the MLP family.}
  \label{fig:tightness}
\end{figure}

The geometric law holds almost exactly (Figure~\ref{fig:tightness}):
the fitted log-log slope of the bound factor matches the predicted
$-1/d$ within $0.03$ in all three dimensions. The measured advantage
respects the bound's ordering, with higher-dimensional, sparser data
more vulnerable at every $n$, and across all 15 $(n,d)$ cells the bound
factor ranks the measured advantage with Spearman $\rho = 0.893$
($p < 10^{-5}$). The advantage also decays at least as fast as the
bound in every dimension, as an upper bound requires; for $d \geq 4$ it
decays faster than $n^{-1/d}$, which is expected because the
loss-threshold attack lower-bounds the Bayes-optimal adversary the
proposition describes. The experiment is self-contained, runs in
minutes on a laptop, and ships with our artifact.

\subsection{Finding 1: Dataset Structure Governs Risk Among Realistic Models}
\label{sec:finding1}

We decompose the variation in MIA AUC by the spread of group-mean AUC across
each factor. Across all nine configurations, dataset choice accounts for
$36\%$ of the variation, model architecture for $39\%$, and the attack
method for $24\%$. Taken at face value, the model appears to rival the
dataset.

This parity is almost entirely an artifact of a single model. The Random
Forest, trained without a depth limit, memorizes its training set and
saturates near AUC $1.0$ on nearly every dataset. Its mean AUC is $0.75$,
against $0.56$ for the MLP and $0.62$ for XGBoost. This is an adversarial
worst case, not a configuration that practitioners deploy. Restricting the
decomposition to the regularized models that are actually used, MLP and
XGBoost, dataset structure rises to $49.5\%$ of the variation, twice the
model share ($24.4\%$) and well ahead of attack ($26.1\%$).

We therefore read Finding~1 as follows. Across realistic models, dataset
structure governs membership inference risk more than the choice of model,
and far more than the choice of attack. A deliberately overfit model can
erase that margin, which is itself a caution. Reporting a single attack
number on a single dataset conflates three distinct sources of variation,
and the dataset is not the incidental one.

\subsection{Finding 2: Benchmarks Are Geometric Outliers}
\label{sec:finding2}

The two most widely used MIA benchmarks, Purchase100 and Texas100, are
geometric outliers among the 31 datasets. Their sample uniqueness ($28.0$
and $55.7$) is the highest of all datasets we study, far above the median
of roughly $1.5$, and their risk ($0.85$ and $0.92$) is likewise the top
two. A model evaluated only on these benchmarks is being attacked in an
unrepresentatively high-risk regime.
The finding has precedent in neighbouring fields: computer vision
documented that canonical benchmarks carry strong dataset-specific
bias~\cite{torralba2011unbiased} and that conclusions drawn on them
shift on fresh data~\cite{recht2019imagenet}, and recent work on large
language models finds that MIA success depends heavily on which data is
evaluated~\cite{duan2024membership}. Our contribution is to give this
effect a measurable geometric cause for MIA benchmarks.

We temper this in two ways. First, the effect is not exclusive to
benchmarks: high-dimensional image datasets such as MNIST and Fashion-MNIST
also have elevated uniqueness, because high dimensionality sparsifies the
data. Second, with only two benchmark datasets a formal distributional test
has little power. The claim we make is therefore concrete and limited:
on the single most important geometric axis, sample uniqueness, the two
canonical MIA benchmarks sit at the extreme of the entire 31-dataset range.
Corollary~\ref{cor:benchmark} explains why subsampled, class-balanced
benchmarks are expected to have inflated uniqueness from first principles.

\subsection{Statistical Power: A Cautionary Tale}
\label{sec:power}

Our unit of statistical analysis is the dataset, so every correlation in
this paper is computed at $n=31$. We close with an observation about what
that sample size buys, because it reframes a limitation as a measurement.

An early version of this study used seven datasets, a corpus already
larger than the two-to-four datasets typical of MIA evaluations. At
$n=7$, the results looked spectacular: Random Forest regression reached a
Spearman correlation of $0.93$, and sample uniqueness alone correlated
perfectly with risk ($\rho = 1.0$). Both numbers were artifacts. When we
grew the corpus to 31 datasets, they collapsed to $0.36$ and $0.506$
respectively. Nothing about the pipeline was wrong at $n=7$; the sample
was simply too small to constrain the hypothesis, and every additional
degree of freedom in the estimator converted noise into apparent signal.

This experience shaped the paper's reporting discipline. We report the
nested cross-validated estimate as the headline because it is the only
number that survives feature-subset selection without optimism. We report
exact $p$-values for every standalone correlation. And we deliberately
characterize the $\rho = 0.61$ result as moderate, because at $n=31$ the
honest claim is a significant ranking signal, not a precise predictor.

The cautionary tale generalizes beyond this paper. If seven datasets are
enough to produce a perfect-looking but spurious correlation, then
conclusions drawn from the two-benchmark evaluations that dominate the
MIA literature rest on far less evidence than their point estimates
suggest. We view the 31-dataset corpus, and the collapse we observed on
the way to it, as independent support for Finding~1's call to treat the
dataset as a first-class experimental factor.

\subsection{Limitations and Threats to Validity}
\label{sec:limitations}

We close the results with the boundaries of what they establish.

\emph{Construct validity.} $\mathrm{Risk}(D)$ is the mean AUC over a
nine-configuration grid, a typical-adversary surrogate rather than the
worst case. We argued in Section~\ref{sec:setup} that the per-dataset
maximum is uninformative because it collapses onto the memorizing
Random Forest everywhere, but other aggregations (a regularized-models
mean, an attack-specific risk) are defensible, and the DPRI correlation
will vary somewhat with that choice. Our attack grid is black-box;
white-box attacks~\cite{nasr2019comprehensive,leino2020stolen} could
shift the ground truth, though they generally strengthen, not reorder,
membership signal.

\emph{Internal validity.} The relationship between geometry and risk is
correlational. We do not intervene on a dataset's geometry while
holding its learning task fixed, so we cannot claim that lowering
uniqueness causally lowers risk; the onion
effect~\cite{carlini2022onion} suggests such interventions have
non-obvious consequences. The six features are also collinear, which is
why we report standalone correlations and a regularized combined model
rather than coefficient-level attributions.

\emph{External validity.} Our corpus is tabular-centric: image data
enters flattened, and text, graph, and time-series data are absent.
Datasets above 30k samples are subsampled, which inflates uniqueness
relative to their full-size deployments; the effect is shared across
datasets but not identical. All training is centralized, and we do not
evaluate DP-SGD, so the data-aware DP direction of
Section~\ref{sec:implications} remains a hypothesis.

\emph{Statistical power.} Every correlation rests on $n = 31$ datasets.
The nested cross-validation removes selection optimism but cannot
shrink confidence intervals; at this sample size the honest claim is a
significant ranking signal, and Section~\ref{sec:power} documents why
we do not claim more.
