\begin{abstract}
% Write abstract LAST — after all results are in.
% Key elements: 1 sentence problem, 3 findings with numbers, 1 sentence implication.
%
A decade of research on membership inference attacks (MIA), attribute
inference, differential privacy, and machine unlearning has produced ever
stronger attacks and defenses, yet a more fundamental question remains
unanswered: why are some datasets inherently more prone to privacy leakage
than others, independent of the model trained on them? This is a question
about the nature of data rather than the engineering of attacks, and it has
direct consequences for how privacy risk is measured and mitigated in
practice.

We argue that membership inference is largely a symptom whose underlying
cause is the geometric structure of the training data. To make this precise,
we introduce the \emph{Dataset Privacy Risk Index} (DPRI), a risk estimator
computed entirely from the raw data distribution---using sample uniqueness,
local density, outlier structure, feature entropy, and class
separability---and requiring no trained model, no shadow models, and no
attack. We ground DPRI in a formal analysis showing that the advantage of an
optimal membership inference adversary is upper-bounded by a function of
these pre-training distributional quantities, providing a principled
explanation for why data geometry, rather than model architecture, should
dominate privacy risk.

Building on this foundation, we investigate three questions of broad
consequence for the security community: whether dataset structure predicts
privacy risk more reliably than the choice of model; whether the benchmarks
that have anchored a decade of MIA evaluation are structurally
representative of the data practitioners actually deploy; and whether the
protection delivered by a fixed differential privacy budget depends on the
underlying data distribution. Together, these questions reframe privacy risk
as a property that can be anticipated before training rather than measured
after deployment. We release DPRI as an open-source tool so that data
holders can audit a dataset's privacy risk at collection time, before
committing to a model or a privacy budget.
\end{abstract}
